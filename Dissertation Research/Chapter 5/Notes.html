The Transfer Process

(9) “The transfer of technology from one society to another, and especially from one civilization to another, is of an altogether higher order of complexity, and no theory has yet emerged to encompass it all. Yet certain basic premises are clear. First of all, the transfer of technology is not one process but two. One of these is the relocation, from one area to another, of equipment and methods, along with the experts to operate them. The other is the diffusion from one society to another of the knowledge, skills, and attitudes related to a particular device or process. Thus we will distinguish the geographic relocation from the cultural diffusion of technology.”

(13) “The cultural diffusion of technology, in contrast, is a much more difficult task. It takes a willingness to accept changes, a strong political cohesiveness, and a common vision of the future. Western societies, facing less culture shock, have readily imported industrial technologies. Among non-Western societies, only Japan had the requisite cultural and social base. Others have had to undergo political revolutions first.”

(14) “Western technology did not reach the tropics in a continuous stream, but in the form of discrete projects: a railway, a plantation, a telegraph network.”

(5) “The cultivation of cash crops on agricultural holdings of all sizes characterizes the region today. The holdings can be divided into estates (100 acres or more) and small holdings (less than 100 acres). The geographical distribution of these holdings is quite regular, due partly to the way in which land was alienated for agriculture.”

(5) “Estates occupy nearly two-thirds of the entire area under cultivation in the region. These range in size from over 3,000 to 150 acres or less. Considerations of accessibility, safe distance from the tidal river, and the availability of larger tracts of contiguous Jand for planting have resulted in the extension of large estates on the southern side of the main road. The estates are invariably monocrop and mostly planted with rubber. In the absence of natural drainage such as obtains on foothill locations, success in rubber planting on this flat coastal land is dependent on an efficient system of artificial drains. Channels on most of the estates keep the water table at an average depth of forty-two inches by draining superfluous water into the Chempaka.”

(6) “All European-owned estates of the region employ a force containing over go percent resident South Indians and a small population of Chinese and Javanese contract laborers. The smaller Chinese estates have an unspecialized and nonresident force also working under a contractor. There is very little subsistence agriculture on estates; almost the only crops of this sort are vegetables, betels, plantains, and manioc grown by the laborers for domestic consumption on patches of land provided by the management.”

  • (206) “For a major part of the prewar industrial subsystem on Pal Melayu, the kangany occupied interdependent roles of recruiter and foreman. The kangany’s role as a recruiter fortified his position as the first-line supervisor of his gang. Conversely, in cases where a recruiter was subsequently raised to the rank of foreman, his ability to recruit coolies was being directly rewarded by the management. Whether the kangany was first a recruiter and then a foreman or vice versa, a system had been established in which the immediate supervision of laborers was linked directly to their recruitment. This basic principle of the industrial subsystem was exemplified in the constitution of work-gangs. The management rewarded the kangany with authority over his gang, no laboring work to perform, and higher wages, all this in exchange for recruiting, retaining, and replenishing the supply of labor.”
  • (207) “On Pal Melayu the autonomy of the head kangany or kanganwas curtailed by making him a part of a carefully organized management bureaucracy.”

In numerous statements culled from the life histories of both old laborers and managers on Pal Melayu, the manager and his European assistants of those days are described as the father and mother of the Tamil coolies. Interestingly enough, from the beginning, these are precisely the terms in which the attitude of the kangany or the head kangany toward the laborers was also epitomized.”
  • (218) “In this phase, the management deliberately designed the structure of human relations with the coolies to conform to the paternalistic principle. In times of such acute need for a stable labor force as the mid- and late 1920s, the manager on Pal Melayu manipulated roles in such a way that for the coolie hand his European assistants became the benevolent father figure while the head kangany and his kanganies represented their punitive counterparts. The capture and chastisement of bolters as well as the field supervision of laborers was entrusted increasingly to the kangany, head kangany, probationer conductor (kanakka-p-pillai), and Asian field conductors.”

  • (231-232) “It should, however, be pointed out that the fall in the kangany’s status in the industrial subsystem was not matched by a decline in his real influence among laborers. In adopting various measures to downgrade the kangany, the management on Pal Melayu seems to have been unduly impressed by the large number of coastal immigrants in the post-Depression labor force who had not originally been recruited by kanganies. This official reckoning overlooked the fact that a large number of recruits migrating under the surat system (p. 226, n. 18) were in fact clients of a kangany. More often than not, these recruits had obtained bearer-letters from the estate manager on the recommendation of a kangany. Moreover, most local recruits—by no means an insignificant proportion of the labor force—were brought in by a kangany. No change in the industrial subsystem could immediately affect the existing networks of kinship and economic ties into which a kangany found it advantageous to enmesh a new recruit.”

Bachelors’ lodges and domestic groups based on nuclear families were thus the two most common types of residential arrangements in the prewar period. True joint households based on patrilateral or patrilineal extension were few in number and short-lived, being confined almost exclusively to newly arrived immigrants.”

In a large majority of cases the person to join was an unmarried cognate or an affine of the household head. Such an arrangement could rarely survive the many quarrels and quibbles that were bound to arise in joint living; it was rare, though not altogether unknown, for this unit to outlast the marriage of the joining kinsman. This applied equally to arrangments in which the joining person was a close kin, such as a sibling, and others in which he was more distantly related. For whatever the period, however, this “joint household” functioned as an effective domestic group. It was essentially a grouping of convenience and mutual aid. A number of men who immigrated to Pal Melayu as adult bachelors entered one or more such arrangements before acquiring wives and setting up their own domestic groups.”

  • (164) “A cittu is a savings group, comprising members who agree to make regular contributions to a fund that is distributed, in whole or in part, to each contributor in rotation.”
  • (165) “Ten men meet every month and contribute one shilling each to a fund which is straight away handed over to one of their members.
